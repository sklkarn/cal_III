    Section 2.3 Motion in Three-Dimensional Space

    In this section, we will explore the motion of objects in three-dimensional space, including velocity and acceleration vectors. Suppose a particle moves through space so that its position vector at time t is \(\mathbf{r(t)}\text{.}\) the velocity vector of the particle is the derivative of the position vector \(\mathbf{r(t)}\) with respect to time \(t\) is \(\mathbf{r'(t)}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \mathbf{r'(t)} = \lim_{\h \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r(t + h)} - \mathbf{r(t)}}{h} \end{equation*}
    Where \(h \) is very small length of time. The acceleration vector of the particle is the derivative of the velocity vector with respect to time \(t\) is \(\mathbf{r''(t)}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(t)} = \mathbf{r''(t)} = \lim_{\h \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r'(t + h)} - \mathbf{r'(t)}}{h} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 2.3.1.

    A particle moves along a curve in space with position vector \(\mathbf{r(t)} = \langle t, t^2, t^3 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the velocity and acceleration vectors at time \(t = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The velocity vector is the derivative of the position vector:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \mathbf{r'(t)} = \langle 1, 2t, 3t^2 \rangle \end{equation*}
    At time \(t = 1\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(1)} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The acceleration vector is the derivative of the velocity vector:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(t)} = \mathbf{r''(t)} = \langle 0, 2, 6t \rangle \end{equation*}
    At time \(t = 1\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(1)} = \langle 0, 2, 6 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.2.

    A particle moves along a curve in space with position vector \(\mathbf{r(t)} = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle\text{.}\) Find the velocity and acceleration vectors at time \(t = \pi/4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The velocity vector is the derivative of the position vector:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \mathbf{r'(t)} = \langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    At time \(t = \pi/4\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(\pi/4)} = \langle -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The acceleration vector is the derivative of the velocity vector:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(t)} = \mathbf{r''(t)} = \langle -\cos t, -\sin t, 0 \rangle \end{equation*}
    At time \(t = \pi/4\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(\pi/4)} = \langle -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.3.

    Find the velocity, acceleration, and speed of a particle with position vector \(\mathbf{r(t)} = \langle t^2, e^t, te^t \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The velocity vector is the derivative of the position vector:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \mathbf{r'(t)} = \langle 2t, e^t, e^t + te^t \rangle \end{equation*}
    The acceleration vector is the derivative of the velocity vector:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(t)} = \mathbf{r''(t)} = \langle 2, e^t, 2e^t + te^t \rangle \end{equation*}
    The speed of the particle is the magnitude of the velocity vector:
    \begin{equation*} \text{speed} = ||\mathbf{v(t)}|| = \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (e^t)^2 + (e^t + te^t)^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.4.

    A moving particle starts at an initial position \(\mathbf{r(0)} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle\) with initial velocity \(\mathbf{v(0)} = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle\text{.}\) Its acceleration is \(\mathbf{a(t)} = \langle 4t, 6t, 1 \rangle\text{.}\) Find its velocity and position at time \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To find the velocity, we integrate the acceleration:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \int \mathbf{a(t)} dt = \int \langle 4t, 6t, 1 \rangle dt = \langle 2t^2 + C_1, 3t^2 + C_2, t + C_3 \rangle \end{equation*}
    Using the initial condition \(\mathbf{v(0)} = \langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle\text{,}\) we find:
    \begin{equation*} C_1 = 1, C_2 = 1, C_3 = 1 \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the velocity is:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \langle 2t^2 + 1, 3t^2 + 1, t + 1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    To find the position, we integrate the velocity:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r(t)} = \int \mathbf{v(t)} dt = \int \langle 2t^2 + 1, 3t^2 + 1, t + 1 \rangle dt = \langle \frac{2}{3}t^3 + t + D_1, t^3 + t + D_2, \frac{1}{2}t^2 + t + D_3 \rangle \end{equation*}
    Using the initial condition \(\mathbf{r(0)} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle\text{,}\) we find:
    \begin{equation*} D_1 = 1, D_2 = 0, D_3 = 0 \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the position is:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r(t)} = \langle \frac{2}{3}t^3 + t + 1, t^3 + t, \frac{1}{2}t^2 + t \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.5.

    A projectile is fired with angle of elevation \(\theta\) and initial velocity \(v_0\text{.}\) Assuming that air resistance is negligible and the only external force is due to gravity, find the position function \(\mathbf{r(t)}\) of the projectile. What value of \(\theta\) maximizes the range (the horizontal distance traveled)?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The position function of the projectile can be modeled as:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r(t)} = \langle v_0 \cos(\theta) t, v_0 \sin(\theta) t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2, 0 \rangle \end{equation*}
    where \(g\) is the acceleration due to gravity. To maximize the range, we need to find the time \(t\_f\) when the projectile hits the ground (y=0):
    \begin{equation*} 0 = v_0 \sin(\theta) t_f - \frac{1}{2} g t_f^2 \end{equation*}
    Solving for \(t_f\text{,}\) we get:
    \begin{equation*} t_f = \frac{2 v_0 \sin(\theta)}{g} \end{equation*}
    The range \(R\) is given by:
    \begin{equation*} R = v_0 \cos(\theta) t_f = v_0 \cos(\theta) \frac{2 v_0 \sin(\theta)}{g} = \frac{v_0^2}{g} 2 \sin(\theta) \cos(\theta) \end{equation*}
    Using the double-angle identity, we have:
    \begin{equation*} R = \frac{v_0^2}{g} \sin(2\theta) \end{equation*}
    To maximize \(R\text{,}\) we need to maximize \(\sin(2\theta)\text{,}\) which occurs when \(2\theta = 90^\circ\text{,}\) or \(\theta = 45^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.1 Tangential and Normal Components of Acceleration

    The acceleration vector of a particle moving along a curve can be decomposed into two components: the tangential component, which is parallel to the velocity vector, and the normal component, which is perpendicular to the velocity vector. The tangential component of acceleration is given by:
    \begin{equation*} a_T = \frac{d||\mathbf{v}||}{dt} \end{equation*}
    and the normal component of acceleration is given by:
    \begin{equation*} a_N = \frac{||\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{a}||}{||\mathbf{v}||} \end{equation*}
    where \(\mathbf{v}\) is the velocity vector and \(\mathbf{a}\) is the acceleration vector.
    🔗
    In terms of curvature \(\kappa\) and speed \(||\mathbf{v}||\text{,}\) the normal component of acceleration can also be expressed as:
    \begin{equation*} a_N = \kappa ||\mathbf{v}||^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 2.3.6.

    A particle moves along a curve in space with position vector \(\mathbf{r(t)} = \langle t, t^2, t^3 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the tangential and normal components of acceleration at time \(t = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The velocity vector is:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(t)} = \mathbf{r'(t)} = \langle 1, 2t, 3t^2 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The acceleration vector is:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(t)} = \mathbf{r''(t)} = \langle 0, 2, 6t \rangle \end{equation*}
    At time \(t = 1\text{,}\) we have:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v(1)} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle, \quad ||\mathbf{v(1)}|| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{14} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{a(1)} = \langle 0, 2, 6 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The tangential component of acceleration is:
    \begin{equation*} a_T = \frac{d||\mathbf{v}||}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(\sqrt{14}) = 0 \end{equation*}
    The normal component of acceleration is:
    \begin{equation*} a_N = ||\mathbf{a} - a_T\hat{\mathbf{v}}|| = ||\langle 0, 2, 6 \rangle - 0\hat{\mathbf{v}}|| = ||\langle 0, 2, 6 \rangle|| = \sqrt{0^2 + 2^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{40} \end{equation*}

    Subsection 2.3.2 Kepler’s Laws of Planetary Motion

    Kepler’s laws describe the motion of planets around the sun. The first law states that planets move in elliptical orbits with the sun at one focus. The second law states that a line segment joining a planet and the sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time. The third law states that the square of the orbital period of a planet is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit.
    🔗
    Mathematically, the second law can be expressed as:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dA}{dt} = \text{constant} \end{equation*}
    where \(A\) is the area swept out by the line segment joining the planet and the sun. The third law can be expressed as:
    \begin{equation*} T^2 \propto a^3 \end{equation*}
    where \(T\) is the orbital period and \(a\) is the semi-major axis of the orbit.
    🔗
    The first law can be expressed in polar coordinates as:
    \begin{equation*} r = \frac{a(1 - e^2)}{1 + e \cos(\theta)} \end{equation*}
    where \(r\) is the distance from the sun, \(a\) is the semi-major axis, \(e\) is the eccentricity of the ellipse, and \(\theta\) is the angle from the closest approach.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} or, \quad r = \frac{ed}{1 + e \cos(\theta)} \end{equation*}
    where \(d=\frac{a(1 - e^2)}{e}\) is the distance from the sun to the closest approach.
