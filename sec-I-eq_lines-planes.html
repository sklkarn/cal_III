    Section 1.3 Equations of Lines and Planes

    The line in xy-plane is determined by a point \(P_1(x_o, y_o, z_o)\) and its direction vector \(\vec{v}\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{v}\) be a vector parallel to the line: \(L\text{,}\) and \(P(x, y, z)\) be an arbitrary point on the line. Let \(\vec{r}_0\) and \(\vec{r}\) be the position vectors of \(P_o\) and \(P\text{,}\) respectively, as shown in the figure below.
    🔗
    From the figure, we can see that the vector \(\vec{a} = \vec{P_oP} = \vec{r} - \vec{r}_0\) is parallel to the direction vector \(\vec{v}\text{.}\) Therefore, there exists a scalar \(t\) such that: \(\vec{r} - \vec{r}_0 = t \vec{v}\text{.}\) This gives the vector equation of the line:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r} = \vec{r}_0 + t \vec{v}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Each value of parameter \(t\) gives the position vector \(\vec{r}\) of a point on the line, \(L\text{.}\) This parametric representation allows us to describe all points on the line using a single parameter \(t\text{.}\) Positive values of \(t\) correspond to points on the line that lie on one side of \(P_o\text{,}\) whereas negative values of \(t\) correspond to points that lie on the other side of \(P_o\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\vec{v} = \langle a,b,c \rangle \text{,}\) the parametric equations of the line can be written in component form as:
    \begin{equation*} \langle x, y, z \rangle = \langle x_o + ta, y_o+tb, z_o +tc \rangle \text{.} \end{equation*}
    or,
    \begin{equation*} x = x_o + ta, \quad y = y_o + tb, \quad z = z_o + tc\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The symmetric form of the line equation can be written as:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x - x_o}{a} = \frac{y - y_o}{b} = \frac{z - z_o}{c}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 1.3.1.

    Find the parametric and symmetric equations of the line passing through the point \(P_o(1, 2, 3)\) and parallel to the vector \(\vec{v} = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The parametric equations of the line are:
    \begin{equation*} x = 1 + 4t, \quad y = 2 + 5t, \quad z = 3 + 6t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The symmetric form of the line equation is:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x - 1}{4} = \frac{y - 2}{5} = \frac{z - 3}{6}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.2.

    Find the parametric and symmetric equations of the line passing through the point \(A(2, 3, 4)\) and point \(B(3, 1, 7)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The direction vector of the line is:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} = \vec{AB} = \langle 3-2, 1-3, 7-4 \rangle = \langle 1, -2, 3 \rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The parametric equations of the line are:
    \begin{equation*} x = 2 + t, \quad y = 3 - 2t, \quad z = 4 + 3t\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The symmetric form of the line equation is:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x - 2}{1} = \frac{y - 3}{-2} = \frac{z - 4}{3}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.1 Skew Lines

    Two lines in space are said to be skew if they are not parallel and do not intersect. Skew lines exist in three-dimensional space and can be described using parametric equations. For example, the lines
    \begin{equation*} L_1: \vec{r}_1(t) = \vec{r}_{1o} + t\vec{v}_1 \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} L_2: \vec{r}_2(s) = \vec{r}_{2o} + s\vec{v}_2 \end{equation*}
    are skew if their direction vectors \(\vec{v}_1\) and \(\vec{v}_2\) are not parallel and the lines do not intersect.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.2 Planes

    A plane in space is a flat, two-dimensional surface that extends infinitely in all directions. A plane is defined by a point \(P_0(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) in the plane and a normal vector to the plane \(\vec{n} = \langle A, B, C \rangle\text{.}\) Let \(P(x, y, z)\) be any point in the plane, and let \(\vec{r_o}\) and \(\vec{r}\) be the position vectors of \(P_0\) and \(P\text{,}\) respectively. Then the vector \(\vec{P_0P} = \vec{r} - \vec{r_o}\) is perpendicular to \(\vec{n}\text{,}\) so their dot product is zero. Hence the vector equation of the plane is:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{n} \cdot (\vec{r} - \vec{r_o}) = 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If \(\vec{n} = \langle a,b,c \rangle\text{,}\) \(\vec{r} = \langle x,y,z \rangle\text{,}\) and \(\vec{r_o} = \langle x_0,y_0,z_0 \rangle\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This is known as the scalar equation of the plane. The general form of the equation of the plane is
    \begin{equation*} ax + by + cz = d\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(d = ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0\text{.}\) For example, the plane
    \begin{equation*} 2x + 3y - 4z = 5 \end{equation*}
    has a normal vector \(\vec{n} = \langle 2, 3, -4 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.3.3.

    Find the equation of the plane that passes through the point \(P_0(1, 2, 3)\) and has a normal vector \(\vec{n} = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the intercepts of the plane.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The equation of the plane is
    \begin{equation*} 4(x - 1) + 5(y - 2) + 6(z - 3) = 0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    or equivalently,
    \begin{equation*} 4x + 5y + 6z = 32\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The intercepts are \((8, 0, 0)\text{,}\) \((0, 32/5, 0)\text{,}\) and \((0, 0, 16/3)\text{.}\) To find the intercepts, set two variables to zero and solve for the remaining variable.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.4.

    Find the equation of the plane that passes through the point \(P(2, 3, 4)\text{,}\) \(Q(5, 6, 7)\text{,}\) and \(R(8, 9, 10)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vectors \(\vec{PQ} = \langle 3, 3, 3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{PR} = \langle 6, 6, 6 \rangle\) are parallel, so the three points are collinear. Therefore, no unique plane passes through all three points.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.5.

    Find the equation of the plane that passes through the point \(P(1,3,2)\text{,}\) \(Q(3,-1,5)\text{,}\) and \(R(5,2,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vectors \(\vec{PQ} = \langle 2, -4, 3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{PR} = \langle 4, -1, -2 \rangle\) are not parallel, so the three points are not collinear. A normal vector to the plane is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{PQ} \times \vec{PR} = \langle 11, 16, 14 \rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the equation of the plane is
    \begin{equation*} 11(x - 1) + 16(y - 3) + 14(z - 2) = 0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    or equivalently,
    \begin{equation*} 11x + 16y + 14z = 75\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.6.

    Find the point at which the line with parametric equations \(x = 2 - 3t\text{,}\) \(y = 2t\text{,}\) and \(z = 4 + t\) intersects the plane \(4x + 5y - 2z = 12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Substituting the parametric equations into the plane equation gives
    \begin{equation*} 4(2 - 3t) + 5(2t) - 2(4 + t) = 12\text{,} \end{equation*}
    which simplifies to
    \begin{equation*} -4t = 12\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, \(t = -3\text{,}\) and the point of intersection is \(x = 2 - 3 (-3) = 11\text{,}\) \(y = 2 (-3) = -6\text{,}\) and \(z = 4 + (-3) = 1\text{,}\) or \((11, -6, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.7.

    Find the angle between the planes \(2x - y + 4z = 10\) and \(x + 2y - 2z = 5\text{.}\) Find symmetric equations for the line of intersection \(L\) of these two planes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The normal vectors to the planes are \(\vec{n}_1 = \langle 2, -1, 4 \rangle\) and \(\vec{n}_2 = \langle 1, 2, -2 \rangle\text{.}\) Therefore, the angle between the planes is
    \begin{align*} \theta \amp = \arccos\left(\frac{\vec{n}_1 \cdot \vec{n}_2}{\|\vec{n}_1\|\|\vec{n}_2\|}\right)\\ \amp = \arccos\left(\frac{2 - 2 - 8}{\sqrt{21}\sqrt{9}}\right)\\ \amp = \arccos\left(\frac{8}{3\sqrt{21}}\right) \approx 0.835 \text{ radians} \end{align*}
    🔗
    A direction vector for the line of intersection is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{n}_1 \times \vec{n}_2 = \langle -6, 8, 5 \rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \vec{n}_1 \times \vec{n}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 2 & -1 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 & -2 \end{vmatrix} = \langle -6, 8, 5 \rangle \end{equation*}
    Setting \(z = 0\) and solving the plane equations gives the point \((5, 0, 0)\) on the line. Therefore, symmetric equations for the line are
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x - 5}{-6} = \frac{y}{8} = \frac{z}{5}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.3.8.

    In three-dimensional space, a single linear equation in \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\) defines a plane. Because two non-parallel planes intersect along a shared path, a system of two linear equations can be used to describe a line. Any coordinate point \((x, y, z)\) that satisfy both \(a_1x + b_1y + c_1z + d_1 = 0\) and \(a_2x + b_2y + c_2z + d_2 = 0\) lie on both of these planes at once, and so the pair of linear equations represents the line of intersection of the planes (if they are not parallel).
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.3.2.1 Distance from a Point to a Plane

    A formula for measuring the distance \(d\) from a point \(P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)\) to the plane \(ax + by + cz + d = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(d\) be the distance from \(P_1\) to the plane. Consider any arbitrary point \(P_o (x_o, y_o, z_o)\) on the plane, so that direction vector
    \begin{equation*} \overrightarrow{P_oP_1}=\vec{v} =(x_1-x_o, y_1-y_o, z_1-z_o)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The scalar projection of \(\vec{v}\) onto the normal vector \(\vec{n}=\langle a,b,c\rangle\) is
    \begin{equation*} d = \left|\vec{v}\cdot \hat{n} \right| = \left|\vec{v}\cdot\frac{\vec{n}}{\|\vec{n}\|}\right| \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{\left|a(x_1-x_o)+b(y_1-y_o)+c(z_1-z_o)\right|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{\left|ax_1+by_1+cz_1-(ax_o+by_o+cz_o)\right|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \therefore \quad d = \frac{\left|ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 + d\right|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 1.3.9.

    Find the distance from the point \(P_1(1, 2, 3)\) to the plane \(2x + 3y + 4z - 5 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the distance formula,
    \begin{equation*} d = \frac{\left|2(1)+3(2)+4(3)-5\right|}{\sqrt{2^2+3^2+4^2}} = \frac{15}{\sqrt{29}} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the distance is \(d = \frac{15}{\sqrt{29}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.10.

    Find the distance from the plane \(2x + 4y + 6z + 3 = 0\) to the plane \(x + 2y + 3z - 7 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To calculate the distance between the planes, first note that their normal vectors are parallel. Therefore, the planes are parallel. Choose a point on the first plane, such as \((0, 0, -\frac{1}{2})\text{.}\) Then calculate the distance from this point to the second plane, using the distance formula,
    \begin{equation*} d = \frac{\left|3+7\right|}{\sqrt{1^2+2^2+3^2}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{14}} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the distance is \(d = \frac{10}{\sqrt{14}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.11.

    Find the distance between the skew lines \(L_1: x=1+t, y=2+3t, z=3+4t \) and \(L_2: x=4+5s, y=5+6s, z=6+7s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The direction vectors of the lines are \(\vec{v}_1 = \langle 1, 3, 4\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}_2 = \langle 5, 6, 7\rangle\text{.}\) A vector perpendicular to both lines is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_1 \times \vec{v}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 1 & 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 7 \end{vmatrix} = \langle -3, 13, -9\rangle \end{equation*}
    The distance between the lines is the distance from \(P_1(1, 2, 3)\) to the plane through \(P_2(4, 5, 6)\) with normal vector \(\vec{v}_1 \times \vec{v}_2\text{.}\) Using the distance formula,
    \begin{gather*} d = \frac{\left|\langle 3, 3, 3\rangle \cdot \langle -3, 13, -9\rangle\right|}{\sqrt{(-3)^2+13^2+(-9)^2}} \\ = \frac{3}{\sqrt{259}} \end{gather*}
    Therefore, the distance is \(d = \frac{3}{\sqrt{259}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.12.

    Find the distance between the skew lines
    \begin{equation*} L_1: \frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y-2}{3} = \frac{z-3}{4} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} L_2: \frac{x-4}{5} = \frac{y-5}{6} = \frac{z-6}{7}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The direction vectors of the lines are \(\vec{v}_1 = \langle 2, 3, 4\rangle\) and \(\vec{v}_2 = \langle 5, 6, 7\rangle\text{.}\) A vector perpendicular to both lines is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_1 \times \vec{v}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 7 \end{vmatrix} = \langle -3, 6, -3\rangle \end{equation*}
    The distance between the lines is the distance from \(P_1(1, 2, 3)\) to the plane through \(P_2(4, 5, 6)\) with normal vector \(\vec{v}_1 \times \vec{v}_2\text{.}\) Using the distance formula,
    \begin{equation*} d = \frac{\left|\langle 3, 3, 3\rangle \cdot \langle -3, 6, -3\rangle\right|}{\sqrt{(-3)^2+6^2+(-3)^2}} = \frac{9}{\sqrt{54}} = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{2} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the distance is \(d = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\text{.}\)
