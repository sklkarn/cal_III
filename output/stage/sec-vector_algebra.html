    Section 1.2 Vector Algebra

    A vector is a quantity that has both magnitude and direction. It is represented by an arrow and a directed line segment. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude of the physical quantity, while its direction indicates the direction of that physical quantity (say displacement) as shown in the figure below. The vector is denoted by \(\vec{OA}\) or \(\vec{a}\text{.}\) Here, \(O\) is the initial point and \(A\) is the terminal point. Since this vector starts at the origin, it is called a position vector.
    🔗
    In this section, we will explore the algebra of vectors in three-dimensional space, including vector addition, subtraction, scalar multiplication, and the dot product.
    🔗
    Two parallel vectors have the same or opposite direction. For example, if \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) are parallel, then \(\vec{b} = c\vec{a}\) for some scalar \(c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A vector can be represented in component form as \(\vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\text{,}\) or \(\vec{a} = a_1 \hat{i}+ a_2 \hat{j}+ a_3 \hat{k},\) where \(a_1\text{,}\) \(a_2\text{,}\) and \(a_3\) are the components of the vector along the \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\) axes, respectively.
    🔗
    The magnitude of a vector \(\vec{a}\) is given by \(\|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A unit vector is a vector with magnitude 1. The unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{a}\) is given by \(\hat{a} = \frac{\vec{a}}{\|\vec{a}\|}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The sum of two vectors \(\vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle\) is given by \(\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \langle a_1 + b_1, a_2 + b_2, a_3 + b_3 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The difference of two vectors \(\vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle\) is given by \(\vec{a} - \vec{b} = \langle a_1 - b_1, a_2 - b_2, a_3 - b_3 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The scalar multiple of a vector \(\vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) by a scalar \(c\) is given by \(c\vec{a} = \langle ca_1, ca_2, ca_3 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The dot product of two vectors \(\vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle\) is given by \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The cross product of two vectors \(\vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle\) is given by \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \langle a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2, a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3, a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.1 Combination of Vectors

    Suppose a vector \(\vec{OA} \) is representing a particle that has displaced from point \(O\) to point \(A\text{;}\) the vector \(\vec{AB}\) represents the displacement from point \(A\) to point \(B\text{,}\) then the total displacement of the particle is given by
    \begin{equation*} \vec{OB} = \vec{OA} + \vec{AB}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This is called the addition of the vectors. In addition to the vectors, a tail-to-head method can be used to find the resultant vector. Here, the tail of \(\vec{AB}\) is placed at the head of \(\vec{OA}\text{,}\) and the resultant vector \(\vec{OC}\) is drawn from the tail of \(\vec{OA}\) to the head of \(\vec{AB}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In the figure above, the vector \(\vec{OB}\) is the resultant vector obtained by adding the vectors \(\vec{OA}\) and \(\vec{AB}\text{.}\) This demonstrates the tail-to-head method for vector addition.
    🔗

    Example 1.2.3.

    Let \(\vec{OA} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle\) and \(\vec{AB} = \langle -1, 2 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the resultant vector \(\vec{OB}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{OB} = \vec{OA} + \vec{AB} = \langle 3, 4 \rangle + \langle -1, 2 \rangle = \langle 2, 6 \rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.4.

    Let \(\vec{a} = \langle 2, 3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle 1, -1 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the sum of these vectors.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \langle 2, 3 \rangle + \langle 1, -1 \rangle = \langle 3, 2 \rangle = \vec{c}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.5.

    Let \(\vec{a} = \langle 4, 5 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle -2, 3 \rangle\text{.}\) Find the subtraction of these vectors.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} - \vec{b} = \langle 4, 5 \rangle - \langle -2, 3 \rangle = \langle 6, 2 \rangle = \vec{c}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Vector addition and subtraction can be performed component-wise. For two vectors \(\vec{a} = \langle a_x, a_y \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle b_x, b_y \rangle\text{,}\) their sum and difference are given by:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \langle a_x + b_x, a_y + b_y \rangle, \quad \vec{a} - \vec{b} = \langle a_x - b_x, a_y - b_y \rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Collinear vectors are vectors that lie on the same line or are parallel to each other.
    • They can point in the same direction or in opposite directions. Their magnitudes do not have to be equal.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Two vectors are collinear if one is a scalar multiple of the other.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Example: If \(\vec{a}= \langle 2, 4, 6 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b}= \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\text{,}\) they are collinear because \(\vec{a}= 2\vec{b}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Coplanar vectors are three or more vectors that lie in the same flat plane (or parallel planes).
    • The Rule of Two: Any two vectors in space are always coplanar because you can always pass a flat sheet of paper through any two intersecting or parallel lines. The concept of coplanarity only becomes interesting and restrictive when dealing with three or more vectors.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Three vectors \(\vec{a}\text{,}\) \(\vec{b}\text{,}\) and \(\vec{c}\) are coplanar if one can be expressed as a linear combination of the other two:
      \begin{equation*} \vec{c}= x\vec{a}+ y\vec{b} \end{equation*}
      where \(x\) and \(y\) are scalars. Alternatively, three vectors are coplanar if their scalar triple product is zero:
      \begin{equation*} \vec{a}\cdot (\vec{b}\times \vec{c}) = 0 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.1.1 Component of a Vector

    A vector can be expressed in component form as an ordered pair of its horizontal and vertical components. For example, the vector \(\vec{v} = \langle v_x, v_y \rangle\) has horizontal component \(v_x\) and vertical component \(v_y\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) vector algebra The horizontal component represents the projection of the vector onto the x-axis, while the vertical component represents the projection onto the y-axis.
    🔗
    In \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{,}\) a vector can be expressed in component form as an ordered triple of its horizontal, vertical, and depth components. For example, the vector \(\vec{v} = \langle v_x, v_y, v_z \rangle\) has horizontal component \(v_x\text{,}\) vertical component \(v_y\text{,}\) and depth component \(v_z\text{.}\) The horizontal component represents the projection of the vector onto the x-axis, the vertical component represents the projection onto the y-axis, and the depth component represents the projection onto the z-axis.
    🔗
    If the vector with representation \(\vec{AB}\) has initial point \(A(x_1, y_1, z_1)\) and terminal point \(B(x_2, y_2, z_2)\text{,}\) then the vector \(\vec{a}\) is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} = \langle x_2 - x_1, y_2 - y_1, z_2 - z_1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 1.2.6.

    Find the vector \(\vec{AB}\) if \(A(1, 2, 3)\) and \(B(4, 5, 6)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{AB} = \langle 4-1, 5-2, 6-3 \rangle = \langle 3, 3, 3 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.7.

    If \(\vec{a} = \langle 2, 0, 3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle -2, 3, 5 \rangle\text{,}\) find \(\vert \vec{a} \vert\) and the vectors \(\vec{a} + \vec{b}\text{,}\) \(\vec{a} - \vec{b}\text{,}\) \(3\vec{b}\text{,}\) and \(2\vec{a} + 5\vec{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vert \vec{a} \vert = \sqrt{2^2 + 0^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{13} \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \langle 2 + (-2), 0 + 3, 3 + 5 \rangle = \langle 0, 3, 8 \rangle \vec{a} - \vec{b} = \langle 2 - (-2), 0 - 3, 3 - 5 \rangle = \langle 4, -3, -2 \rangle 3\vec{b} = \langle 3(-2), 3(3), 3(5) \rangle = \langle -6, 9, 15 \rangle 2\vec{a} + 5\vec{b} = \langle 2(2) + 5(-2), 2(0) + 5(3), 2(3) + 5(5) \rangle = \langle -6, 15, 31 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.1.2 Unit Vector

    A unit vector is a vector with magnitude \(1\text{.}\) Unit vectors are often used to indicate direction without considering magnitude. For any nonzero vector \(\vec{v}\text{,}\) the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{v}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \hat{v} = \frac{\vec{v}}{\vert \vec{v} \vert}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    For example, if \(\vec{v} = \langle 3, 4, 0 \rangle\text{,}\) then \(\vert \vec{v} \vert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 0^2} = 5\text{,}\) and the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{v}\) is \(\hat{v} = \frac{1}{5}\langle 3, 4, 0 \rangle = \langle \frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5}, 0 \rangle\text{.}\) Unit vectors in the positive coordinate directions are
    \begin{equation*} \hat{i} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle \quad \hat{j} = \langle 0, 1, 0 \rangle \quad \hat{k} = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    These unit vectors are called the standard basis vectors.
    🔗

    Example 1.2.8.

    If \(\vec{a} = \hat{i} + 5\hat{j} - 3\hat{k}\) and \(\vec{b} = 2\hat{i} - 5\hat{k}\text{,}\) express the vector \(2\vec{a} + 3\vec{b}\) in terms of \(\hat{i}\text{,}\) \(\hat{j}\text{,}\) and \(\hat{k}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} 2\vec{a} + 3\vec{b} = 2(\hat{i} + 5\hat{j} - 3\hat{k}) + 3(2\hat{i} - 5\hat{k}) \\ \amp = (2 + 6)\hat{i} + 10\hat{j} + (-6 - 15)\hat{k} \\ \amp = 8\hat{i} + 10\hat{j} - 21\hat{k} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.9.

    Find the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{v} =2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \vert \vec{v} \vert = \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + (-6)^2} = 7 \hat{v}= \frac{1}{7}(2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k}) = \frac{2}{7}\hat{i} + \frac{3}{7}\hat{j} - \frac{6}{7}\hat{k} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.2.10.

    The tangent line to a curve at a point has slope \(m\text{,}\) so a direction vector for the tangent line is \(\vec{v} = \langle 1, m \rangle\text{.}\) A vector perpendicular to the tangent line is \(\vec{w} = \langle 1, -1/m \rangle\) or \(\vec{w} = \langle -m, 1 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.11.

    Find the unit vectors that are parallel to the tangent line to the parabola \(y = x^2\) at the point \((3, 9)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The slope of the tangent line is \(m=y'(3) = 6\text{,}\) so a direction vector for the tangent line is \(\vec{v} = \langle 1, 6 \rangle\text{.}\) Since \(\vert \vec{v} \vert = \sqrt{1^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{37}\text{,}\) the unit vectors parallel to the tangent line are
    \begin{equation*} \pm \hat{v} = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{37}}\langle 1, 6 \rangle = \left\langle \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{37}}, \pm \frac{6}{\sqrt{37}} \right\rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.12.

    Find the unit vectors that are parallel to the tangent line to the curve \(y = 2\sin x\) at the point \((\pi/6, 1)\text{.}\) Find the unit vectors that are perpendicular to the tangent line.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The slope of the tangent line is \(m=y'(\pi/6) = \sqrt{3}\text{,}\) so a direction vector for the tangent line is \(\vec{v} = \langle 1, m \rangle= \langle 1, \sqrt{3} \rangle\text{.}\) Since \(\vert \vec{v} \vert = \sqrt{1^2 + (\sqrt{3})^2} = 2\text{,}\) the unit vectors parallel to the tangent line are
    \begin{equation*} \pm \hat{v} = \pm \frac{1}{2}\langle 1, \sqrt{3} \rangle = \left\langle \pm \frac{1}{2}, \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right\rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A vector perpendicular to \(\vec{v}\) is \(\vec{w} =\langle 1, -1/m \rangle= \langle 1, -1/\sqrt{3} \rangle\text{.}\) Since \(\vert \vec{w} \vert = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1/\sqrt{3})^2} = 2/\sqrt{3}\text{,}\) the unit vectors perpendicular to the tangent line are
    \begin{equation*} \pm \hat{w} = \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\langle 1, -1/\sqrt{3} \rangle = \left\langle \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \mp \frac{1}{2} \right\rangle\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.2 Dot Product of Vectors

    If \(a = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) and \(b = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle\text{,}\) then the dot product of \(a\) and \(b\) is the number \(a \cdot b\) given by
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot b = \vert a \vert \vert b \vert \cos \theta = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 \end{equation*}
    here \(\theta\) is the angle between \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    It is a scalar value and has the following properties:
    • Commutative: \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a}\)
      🔗
    • Distributive: \(\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{a} \cdot \vec{c}\)
      🔗
    • Scalar multiplication: \((c\vec{a}) \cdot \vec{b} = c(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})\)
      🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a}\cdot \vec{a} = \vert \vec{a} \vert^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 1.2.13.

    If the vectors \(a\) and \(b\) have lengths 4 and 6, and the angle between them is \(\pi/3\text{,}\) find \(a \cdot b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot b = \vert a \vert \vert b \vert \cos \theta = 4 \cdot 6 \cdot \cos \frac{\pi}{3} = 12 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.2.14.

    If \(\theta\) is the angle between the nonzero vectors \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \cos \theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\vert a \vert \vert b \vert} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.15.

    Find the angle between the vectors \(a = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\) and \(b = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, compute the dot product and the magnitudes:
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot b = 1\cdot4 + 2\cdot5 + 3\cdot6 = 32 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \vert a \vert = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{14} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \vert b \vert = \sqrt{4^2 + 5^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{77} \end{equation*}
    Then,
    \begin{equation*} \cos \theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\vert a \vert \vert b \vert} = \frac{32}{\sqrt{14}\sqrt{77}} = \frac{32}{\sqrt{1078}} \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{32}{\sqrt{1078}}\right) \approx 0.3281 \text{ radians} \text{ (or } 18.8^\circ \text{)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Corollary 1.2.16.

    Two vectors \(a\) and \(b\) are orthogonal (perpendicular) if and only if \(a \cdot b = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.17.

    Show that the vectors \(a = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\) and \(b = \langle -2, 1, 0 \rangle\) are orthogonal.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Compute the dot product:
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot b = 1\cdot(-2) + 2\cdot1 + 3\cdot0 = -2 + 2 + 0 = 0 \end{equation*}
    Since the dot product is zero, the vectors are orthogonal.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.3 Direction Angles and Direction Cosines

    The direction angles of a vector are the angles it makes with the positive coordinate axes, and the direction cosines are the cosines of these angles.
    \begin{equation*} \cos \alpha = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \hat{i}}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} = \frac{a_1}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \cos \beta = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \hat{j}}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} = \frac{a_2}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \cos \gamma = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \hat{k}}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} = \frac{a_3}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2 \alpha + \cos^2 \beta + \cos^2 \gamma = 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle = \vert \vec{a} \vert \langle \cos \alpha, \cos \beta, \cos \gamma \rangle \end{equation*}
    or,
    \begin{equation*} \hat{a} = \frac{\vec{a}}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} = \langle \cos \alpha, \cos \beta, \cos \gamma \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.3.1 Projections

    Scalar projection is the length of the projection of one vector onto another. Mathematically, the scalar projection of \(\vec{a}\) onto \(\vec{b}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \text{comp}_{\vec{b}} \vec{a} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{\vert \vec{b} \vert} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Vector projection is the projection of one vector onto another as a vector. Mathematically, the vector projection of \(\vec{a}\) onto \(\vec{b}\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \text{proj}_{\vec{b}} \vec{a} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{\vert \vec{b} \vert^2} \vec{b} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \text{proj}_{\vec{b}} \vec{a} = \left(\text{comp}_{\vec{b}} \vec{a}\right) \hat{b} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 1.2.18.

    Find the direction cosines of the vector \(\vec{a} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, compute the magnitude:
    \begin{equation*} \vert \vec{a} \vert = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{14} \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \hat{a} = \frac{\vec{a}}{\vert \vec{a} \vert} = \left\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{14}}, \frac{2}{\sqrt{14}}, \frac{3}{\sqrt{14}} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    Thus, the direction cosines are
    \begin{equation*} \cos \alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{14}}, \quad \cos \beta = \frac{2}{\sqrt{14}}, \quad \cos \gamma = \frac{3}{\sqrt{14}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.19.

    Find the scalar and vector projections of \(\vec{a} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\) onto \(\vec{b} = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, compute the dot product and the magnitude of \(\vec{b}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 6 = 32 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \vert \vec{b} \vert = \sqrt{4^2 + 5^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{77} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the scalar projection is
    \begin{equation*} \text{comp}_{\vec{b}} \vec{a} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{\vert \vec{b} \vert} = \frac{32}{\sqrt{77}} \end{equation*}
    The vector projection is
    \begin{equation*} \text{proj}_{\vec{b}} \vec{a} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{\vert \vec{b} \vert^2} \vec{b} = \frac{32}{77} \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle = \left\langle \frac{128}{77}, \frac{160}{77}, \frac{192}{77} \right\rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 1.2.3.2 Exercises

    1.

    Find the dot product of the following vectors:
    1. \(\vec{a} = \langle -2, 3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle 2, 5 \rangle\)
      🔗
    2. \(\vec{a} = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\) and \(\vec{b} = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\)
      🔗

    Subsection 1.2.4 Cross Product of Vectors

    The cross product of two vectors is a vector that is perpendicular to both of the original vectors. It is denoted by \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}\) and has a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram formed by \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\text{.}\) The direction of the cross product is determined by the right-hand rule. Spread your right hand fingers in the direction of \(\vec{a}\text{,}\) then curl them toward \(\vec{b}\text{;}\) your thumb points in the direction of \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(a\) and \(b\) be the two vectors then
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \vert \vec{a} \vert \vert \vec{b} \vert \sin \theta \vec{n}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) and \(\vec{n}\) is a unit vector perpendicular to both \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(a = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle\) and \(b = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle\text{,}\) then the cross product of \(a\) and \(b\) is the vector
    \begin{equation*} a \times b = \langle a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2, a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3, a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The cross product can be easily computed using the determinant of a square matrix:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = (a_2 b_3 - b_2 a_3)\hat{i} - (a_1 b_3 - b_1 a_3)\hat{j} + (a_1 b_2 - b_1 a_2)\hat{k} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Corollary 1.2.20.

    Two nonzero vectors \(a\) and \(b\) are parallel if and only if \(a \times b = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 1.2.21.

    \begin{equation*} \hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k} \\ \hat{j} \times \hat{k} = \hat{i} \\ \hat{k} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j} \\ \hat{j} \times \hat{i} = -\hat{k} \\ \hat{k} \times \hat{j} = -\hat{i} \\ \hat{i} \times \hat{k} = -\hat{j} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.4.1 Scalar Triple Product of Vectors

    The scalar triple product of three vectors \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) is the scalar
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot (b \times c)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    It gives the volume of the parallelepiped formed by \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\text{.}\) The scalar triple product is also equal to the determinant of the matrix formed by the three vectors as its rows:
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot (b \times c) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.4.2 Vector Triple Product of Vectors

    The vector triple product of three vectors \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) is the vector
    \begin{equation*} a \times (b \times c)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    It can be computed using the formula:
    \begin{equation*} a \times (b \times c) = (a \cdot c)b - (a \cdot b)c \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.22.

    Find the cross product of the vectors \(a = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\) and \(b = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\) and verify that it is orthogonal to both vectors.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The cross product is
    \begin{equation*} a \times b = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{vmatrix} = -3\hat{i} + 6\hat{j} - 3\hat{k} \end{equation*}
    Thus, \(a \times b = \langle -3, 6, -3 \rangle\text{.}\) To verify orthogonality, we compute
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot (a \times b) = 1(-3) + 2(6) + 3(-3) = 0 \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} b \cdot (a \times b) = 4(-3) + 5(6) + 6(-3) = 0 \end{equation*}
    Therefore, \(a \times b\) is orthogonal to both \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.23.

    Compute the vector triple product \(a \times (b \times c)\) for the vectors \(a = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\text{,}\) \(b = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\text{,}\) and \(c = \langle 7, 8, 9 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the vector triple product formula, we have
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot c = 1(7) + 2(8) + 3(9) = 50 \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot b = 1(4) + 2(5) + 3(6) = 32 \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} a \times (b \times c) = 50b - 32c = 50\langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle - 32\langle 7, 8, 9 \rangle = \langle 64, 114, 132 \rangle \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.24.

    Compute the scalar triple product \(a \cdot (b \times c)\) for the vectors \(a = \langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle\text{,}\) \(b = \langle 4, 5, 6 \rangle\text{,}\) and \(c = \langle 7, 8, 9 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The scalar triple product is
    \begin{equation*} a \cdot (b \times c) = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{vmatrix} = 1(5 \cdot 9 - 6 \cdot 8) - 2(4 \cdot 9 - 6 \cdot 7) + 3(4 \cdot 8 - 5 \cdot 7) = 0 \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the vectors \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(c\) are coplanar.

    Example 1.2.25.

    Find two unit vectors orthogonal to both \(a = \langle 3, 2, 1 \rangle\) and \(b = \langle -1, 1, 0 \rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The cross product is
    \begin{equation*} a \times b = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = -\hat{i} - \hat{j} + 5\hat{k} \end{equation*}
    Thus, \(a \times b = \langle -1, -1, 5 \rangle\text{.}\) The magnitude is
    \begin{equation*} \|a \times b\| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + (-1)^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the two unit vectors are
    \begin{equation*} \pm \frac{a \times b}{\|a \times b\|} = \pm \left\langle -\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}, -\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}, \frac{5}{3\sqrt{3}} \right\rangle \end{equation*}

    Example 1.2.26.

    Find the area of the parallelogram with vertices \(A = (-2, 1)\text{,}\) \(B = (0, 4)\text{,}\) \(C = (4, 2)\text{,}\) and \(D = (2, -1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vectors from \(A\) to the adjacent vertices are
    \begin{equation*} \vec{AB} = \langle 2, 3, 0 \rangle, \quad \vec{AD} = \langle 4, -2, 0 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The cross product is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{AB} \times \vec{AD} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 2 & 3 & 0 \\ 4 & -2 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = -16\hat{k} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the area of the parallelogram is
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\vec{AB} \times \vec{AD}\right\| = 16 \end{equation*}

    🔗

    Example 1.2.27.

    Find the volume of the parallelepiped with vertices \(A = (0, 0, 0)\text{,}\) \(B = (1, 0, 0)\text{,}\) \(C = (0, 1, 0)\text{,}\) and \(D = (0, 0, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vectors from \(A\) to the adjacent vertices are
    \begin{equation*} \vec{AB} = \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle, \quad \vec{AC} = \langle 0, 1, 0 \rangle, \quad \vec{AD} = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle \end{equation*}
    The volume of the parallelepiped is the absolute value of the scalar triple product:
    \begin{equation*} \left| \vec{AB} \cdot (\vec{AC} \times \vec{AD}) \right| = \left| \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \right| = 1 \end{equation*}
