    Section 1.1 3D Coordinate System

    The Three-Dimensional Coordinate Systems: To locate a point in three-dimensional space, we need a third axis the \(z\)-axis which is perpendicular to both the \(x\)-axis and the \(y\)-axis.
    1. The Origin (\(O\)): The point where the three axes intersect, denoted as \((0, 0, 0)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The Right-Hand Rule: To maintain consistency in standard mathematical orientation, the axes follow the right-hand rule. If you curl the fingers of your right hand from the positive \(x\)-axis toward the positive \(y\)-axis, your thumb points in the direction of the positive \(z\)-axis (typically pointing straight up or out of the page).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Coordinate Planes: The three axes intersect in pairs to form three coordinate planes:
      • The \(xy\)-plane (\(z = 0\)).
        🔗
        🔗
      • The \(yz\)-plane (\(x = 0\)).
        🔗
        🔗
      • The \(xz\)-plane (\(y = 0\)).
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    4. These planes divide space into regions analogous to the quadrants of 2D geometry.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    From Quadrants to Octants: In a two-dimensional plane, the \(x\) and \(y\) axes divide the space into four quadrants, determined by the signs of the coordinates:
    • Quadrant I: \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Quadrant II: \(x < 0\) and \(y > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Quadrant III: \(x < 0\) and \(y < 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Quadrant IV: \(x > 0\) and \(y < 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    In three-dimensional space, the three coordinate planes divide space into eight octants, determined by the signs of the coordinates:
    • Octant I: \(x > 0\text{,}\) \(y > 0\text{,}\) and \(z > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant II: \(x < 0\text{,}\) \(y > 0\text{,}\) and \(z > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant III: \(x < 0\text{,}\) \(y < 0\text{,}\) and \(z > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant IV: \(x > 0\text{,}\) \(y < 0\text{,}\) and \(z > 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant V: \(x > 0\text{,}\) \(y > 0\text{,}\) and \(z < 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant VI: \(x < 0\text{,}\) \(y > 0\text{,}\) and \(z < 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant VII: \(x < 0\text{,}\) \(y < 0\text{,}\) and \(z < 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Octant VIII: \(x > 0\text{,}\) \(y < 0\text{,}\) and \(z < 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In two-dimensional analytical geometry, the graph of an equation involving x and y is a curve in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) In three-dimensional analytical geometry, an equation in x, y, and z represents a surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The Rule of Dimensions (Degrees of Freedom): In analytic geometry, there is a general rule relating the dimension of the space to the number of constraints (equations) imposed:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Dimension of the Graph}= (\text{Dimension of the Ambient Space}) - (\text{Number of Independent Equations}) \end{equation*}
    Let’s see how this rule plays out in both worlds:
    • In two-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{,}\) one independent equation reduces the dimension by one, resulting in a one-dimensional curve. For example, the equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) represents a circle in \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • In three-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{,}\) one independent equation reduces the dimension by one, resulting in a two-dimensional surface. For example, the equation \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\) represents a sphere in \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.1.

    How can you graph the equation \(x = 5\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) space?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{,}\) the equation \(x = 5\) represents a vertical line at \(x = 5\text{.}\) In \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{,}\) the equation \(x = 5\) represents a plane parallel to the \(yz\)-plane at \(x = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.2.

    Plot \(x^2+y^2=1\) at \(z=5\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) space?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{,}\) the equation \(x^2+y^2=1\) at \(z=5\) represents a circle centered at the \(z=5\) with radius 1. In \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{,}\) the equation \(x^2+y^2=1\) represents a cylinder centered on the \(z\)-axis with radius 1.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.1.3.

    Draw the equation \(y =x\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    In \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{,}\) the equation \(y =x\) represents a plane containing the \(z\)-axis and the line \(y =x\) in the \(xy\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.1 Distance between Points

    The distance between two points \(P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)\) and \(P_2(x_2, y_2, z_2)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is given by the formula \(d(P_1, P_2) = \vert P_1P_2\vert = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}.\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.2 Sphere

    A sphere in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is the set of all points that are a fixed distance (radius) from a given point (center). The equation of a sphere with center \((h, k, l)\) and radius \(r\) is \((x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 + (z - l)^2 = r^2.\)
    🔗

    Example 1.1.4.

    Draw the spherical shell \(1 \le x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 4\) with \(z \le 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The spherical shell is the region between two concentric spheres of radii 1 and 2, restricted to the lower hemisphere.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
